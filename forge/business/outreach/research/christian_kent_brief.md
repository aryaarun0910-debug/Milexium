## 4. His published positions — including the one that cuts against us

### 4.1 The flagship piece

**"Have We Outgrown Paratransit?"**, Christian T. Kent, *Mass Transit* magazine, **18 April 2023** — [masstransitmag.com](https://www.masstransitmag.com/bus/paratransit/article/53042943/have-we-outgrown-paratransit) **[V]**

The piece has been picked up and republished/linked by disability-mobility commentators — e.g. [On Foot, On Wheels](https://onfootonwheels.squarespace.com/accessibility/have-we-outgrown-paratransit) **[V]**.

### 4.2 🚩 THE CONTRADICTION — read this before drafting anything

**In the same 2023 article he argues that improving the algorithms inside the existing paratransit model is exactly the wrong move.** Verbatim **[V]**:

> "We have been motivated to apply sophisticated technology not previously available to resolve long-standing paratransit problems."

> "Efforts to perfect this version of service also fall short – now we have technology that more precisely informs the customer of how late the ride will be. **That applies updated algorithms to the same population of trips to be scheduled, still with a 'next day' orientation…**"

His published position: better algorithms applied to the legacy next-day paratransit trip population are **incremental polish on a 1990s artifact**. The fix he argues for is structural — same-day microtransit, accessibility designed into the mainline system, paratransit demoted to a minimal backstop.

**Why this is the single most important finding.** His reply to us was generous — he suggested our work "could lead to further improvement to the algorithms being used by the latest scheduling software products." That is his courtesy, framed in our vocabulary. **It is not his thesis.** If we write back leaning into "we make paratransit scheduling algorithms better", we are walking straight into the argument he published under his own name three years ago, and he will read us as one more vendor polishing the artifact. He may be too gracious to say so, and we would never learn why the thread went cold.

**How to turn it from a risk into the strongest thing in our reply.** Do not soften his position; adopt it and go one level up. The framing that survives his own argument:

- An **auditable evidence layer is model-agnostic**. It measures what actually happened to a trip — booked, offered, denied, negotiated, late, deadheaded — regardless of whether that trip was served by legacy paratransit, comingled microtransit, or a TNC. That is not algorithm-polish; it is the instrumentation you need to *prove* the structural change he advocates is working.
- His own argument creates the need. If an agency shifts trips from paratransit into same-day microtransit, **it must demonstrate to FTA, to its accessibility advisory committee and to plaintiffs' counsel that service to riders with disabilities did not degrade.** Today most agencies cannot show that with a reproducible record. That is a gap his thesis opens and does not close — and it is exactly where an evidence layer sits.
- He explicitly rejects trip-by-trip capacity rationing (*"trip time negotiation, which is the notion that eligibility for the service can be restricted on a trip-by-trip basis"*, *"restricting transit use should not be our objective"*) **[V]**. Any efficiency claim we make must be framed as **releasing capacity**, never as suppressing or rationing demand. Framing efficiency as "fewer trips served" would be an own goal.
- He notes regulators now expect requirements read *"more in the customer's favor"* and that *"there is nothing in the regulations that prohibits going above and beyond minimum requirements"* **[V]**. So pitch the evidence layer as enabling agencies to **prove they exceeded the floor**, not merely to prove they cleared it. Compliance-as-ceiling language will read as small to him.

## 6. Where we could be genuinely useful to him — mapped to his actual service lines

He sells credibility. His deliverables are on-site reviews, scopes of work, evaluation criteria and expert judgement. Every one of those is currently produced from agency-supplied data he has no independent way to verify. That is the seam.

**1. Defensible denial and capacity-constraint records for his compliance reviews.**
His compliance product covers *"regulatory and contract compliance; adherence to key performance indicators; policy efficacy (i.e., eligibility, appeals, and Title VI issues)"* **[V]**. The hardest thing in that work is that "capacity constraint" under the ADA is a **pattern-or-practice** finding — it needs a defensible time series, not a snapshot, and agencies' own denial counts are notoriously definition-dependent (a "negotiated" trip outside the window may or may not be recorded as a denial). An immutable, reproducible record of what was requested, what was offered, and what was accepted — with the definition pinned and versioned — turns his finding from *"the agency told me X"* into *"here is the derivation, re-runnable by anyone."* That is directly saleable by him.

**2. Peer benchmarking that survives challenge, for his operations reviews.**
He reviews *"cost effectiveness"* and KPI adherence **[V]**. Consultants routinely get pushed back on with "our peers aren't comparable." A transparent, sourced peer set — with the comparability logic visible rather than asserted — is a defensive weapon in a client meeting. Concretely: the derived per-agency figures we already hold (e.g. PSTA $52.68/trip vs CCCTA $76.12/trip on comparable DR volumes) are the kind of thing he would otherwise have to build by hand for every engagement.

**3. Reproducible analysis for the litigation-adjacent work.**
He has *"settled countless … class-action litigation related to the provision of ADA paratransit service"* **[R]**, and his strategic partner Delta Services Group offers expert-witness services **[R]**. Expert analysis that cannot be independently re-derived is the weakest link in any deposition. An analysis layer where every number carries its lineage back to the source record is worth more to an expert witness than to almost anyone else in this industry. **Do not assert we know he does expert-witness work — ask.**

**4. Evaluation criteria that vendors can actually be held to — his procurement work.**
This is the highest-leverage item and the one most aligned with his own thesis. He writes *"scope of work; evaluation criteria and procurement methodology"* for paratransit solicitations **[V]**. Today those RFPs largely ask vendors to *assert* on-time performance and denial handling. If the scope required an **auditable, exportable evidence record** as a deliverable — so the agency can verify the vendor's numbers rather than accept them — that raises the floor for every agency he writes for, and it is a piece of language he owns and controls. If we ever become useful to him, this is how.

**5. Instrumentation for the transition he actually advocates.**
Per §4.2: his published argument is comingling and structural replacement, not algorithmic polish. An agency shifting trips from paratransit to same-day microtransit takes on real legal and political exposure and currently has weak means of proving service to disabled riders did not degrade. Offering the measurement layer for *his* preferred future — rather than a better engine for the model he is arguing against — is the framing most likely to land.

**6. Something we can give with nothing asked back.**
He is a one-to-nine-person shop competing for SME seats on WSP and Kimley-Horn bid teams **[V]**. The cheapest genuinely useful thing we can hand him is a small, clean, correctly-sourced piece of analysis he can use in his own work and cite — no logo, no strings, no ask.
