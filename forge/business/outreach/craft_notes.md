# What the cold-outreach craft actually says (researched, not assumed)

Notes from primary practitioner sources and published data studies, kept here
so the copy rules are traceable rather than folklore. Where a widely-repeated
rule turned out to be wrong, that is recorded too — believing a myth costs as
much as ignoring a real rule.

## 1. Length: shorter than I was writing

- Emails of **150+ words are 42% less likely to get a reply** than emails under
  50 words (Lavender, reviewing cold emails at volume).
- **50–125 words** is the highest-replying band across major studies (~8.2%).
  Several 2026 analyses put the true sweet spot **under 80**.
- A cold email is looked at for about **9 seconds**.

→ My v2 drafts were 84–89 words. Cutting to **55–70** is strictly better.

## 2. Subject lines: my biggest single miss

- **All-lowercase beats Title Case by 21%** in cold email — it reads like an
  internal note from a colleague, not a marketer.
- **2–4 words hits ~46% open rates**; performance falls off past 7 words.
- **≤33 characters** is the only length that renders fully on every mobile
  client.
- Marketers never send one-word subjects, which is exactly why short ones look
  internal.

→ Mine were 7 words, 36–40 chars, Title Case ("TCRTA vs the 171 operators your
size"). Wrong on all three counts. `8th of 171` and `tactic 4.3` are right.

## 3. Ditch the pitch, poke the bear (Josh Braun)

- **Don't pitch — make an observation, then ask a neutral question** that gets
  them thinking differently about their current situation.
- **Avoid leading questions.** They put the prospect on the defensive because
  they can "smell your commission breath."
- The underlying principle: *the best way to persuade is to let other people
  persuade themselves.*
- Skip pleasantries entirely — no "hope this finds you well," no "I know you're
  busy."

→ My drafts pitched: *"I build demand forecasting for paratransit. I'll show
you where those hours sit."* That's a pitch with a bow on it. The observation
should carry the value and the question should be neutral.

## 4. CTA: interest-based, never a meeting ask, never more than one

- **Interest-based CTAs ("Curious?", "Want it?") hit ~30% success — twice any
  other type.**
- **Asking for a meeting triggers loss aversion**: the recipient prices 15–30
  minutes with a stranger and declines.
- **Single-ask CTAs lift replies 35–42%** over emails with multiple asks.
- Correct sequencing: low-friction interest ask on touch 1; the meeting ask
  comes later, once there is intent.

→ "Worth 15 minutes?" is a meeting ask and was the weakest line I wrote.

## 5. Curiosity gap

Give enough to provoke, not enough to satisfy. Formula that recurs:
**industry pattern + their specific context + curiosity gap.** The first
sentence must prove you know something real about *them*.

→ My drafts handed over the rank, the median and the dollar value in one go —
fully satisfying the curiosity and leaving nothing to reply for.

## 6. Spam words: mostly a myth — I nearly over-corrected

- I was about to strip "free" from every draft. The data says don't bother:
  in a 774,828-email 2026 analysis, messages containing "free" hit spam
  **24.28%** of the time versus a ~22.5% baseline — **1.7 points**, i.e. noise.
- Word-level keyword scanning stopped being the primary spam mechanism years
  ago; the trigger-word checklists still circulating solve a problem that no
  longer exists in that form.
- **The real 2026 risk factors are structural:** all-caps subject lines,
  multiple exclamation marks, **3+ links in the body**, image-heavy HTML, and
  **attachments in the first email**.
- The one place "free" genuinely hurts: **ALL CAPS in a subject line** — 82%
  spam rate in one 2026 audit.

→ Keep "free" in the body where it is the honest word for what is on offer.
Never in the subject. Our drafts are plain text with zero links and no
attachments, which is what actually matters.
